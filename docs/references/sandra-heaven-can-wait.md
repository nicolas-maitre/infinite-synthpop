**Closest to The Different Story**, and only in the harmony.

- **The same minor world, a semitone apart.** DS is in E minor; HCW is in E♭ minor tuned 29 cents
  sharp, so it sounds 71 cents under DS.
- **The same tempo.** HCW's chorus is 0.6% under DS (119.1 vs 119.8).
- **The same chord vocabulary.**
  - i and VII move against each other: DS's chorus is i–III–VII–iv, and HCW's is i–VII.
  - Both use the minor iv and the major VI.
  - Both put the major V of harmonic minor at the end of a line and cadence it to i. That is the
    "continental Euro pull" `analysis.md` §3 credits to DS: B major in DS's verse, B♭ (with its
    sus4) in HCW's chorus.
  - Both brighten the key with a major IV: DS in its ii–IV turnaround, HCW across its whole verse.
- **The same people.** Kemmler co-wrote both, and Cretu produced both, in 1988–89.

**But the rhythm section shares nothing with any synthpop reference.**

- Every synthpop reference puts the kick on every beat; HCW never does.
- All three synthpop basses play continuous 8ths; HCW's rests on beat 2 and syncopates around the
  backbeat.
- Its kit is a backbeat kit with an interlocking chorus kick, closer to US dance-pop and funk than
  to TF's sequenced electro or DS's.
- The form adds a pre-chorus and a bare verse, which TF and DS don't have.

### Against the Italo disco analyses (in progress)

The Italo files in progress (Valerie Dore's "The Night" and "Get Closer", Clio's "Faces", on their
own branches) measure:

- 110.3, 112.3 and 120.1 BPM;
- a **kick on every beat** in all three, from the first bar to the fade;
- **root-8th basses** in all three ("Faces" also staccato root 8ths);
- minimal fills (none at all in "The Night").

HCW shares only the minor key and the tempo band. It is not Italo by these measurements.

### Verdict

**A separate Euro-disco genre, with HCW as one of its anchors, not synthpop and not Italo.**

- **Why not synthpop:** the generator blends drum traits and bass styles between a genre's
  references (§7 of `analysis.md`, issue #1). Blending HCW's backbeat kick and rest-on-2 riffs with
  TF's four-on-the-floor and 8th-note shared figure would make grooves no reference has. Its
  rhythm section would add a third family to the genre, not a corner of it.
- **Why not Italo:** same reason, more strongly. Italo here means four-on-the-floor and root 8ths.
- **Why Euro-disco:** HCW brings a coherent, separate set of building blocks:
  - section tempos;
  - a backbeat kit;
  - riff basses;
  - verse / pre-chorus / 14-bar chorus;
  - addition-based arrangement.
- **The bridge:** **The Different Story is the bridge.** Harmonically, HCW sits right next to it.
  If the genres share a progression bank, DS's minor-key turns (i–VII, iv, VI, V at the line end)
  and HCW's are close enough to share entries, and the Cretu/Kemmler link explains why.
- **What decides it:** whether "Crazy Juliet", "Innocent Love" and "Self Control" have the same
  rhythm section. If the two other Sandra songs share HCW's kick and bass habits, the genre is
  "Sandra/Cretu Euro-disco", with HCW its Dorian-verse corner.

### New building blocks it needs

1. **Section tempos.** Verse and pre-chorus at the song's base tempo, chorus about +1.1%,
   switching on the section's downbeat. The tempo would move from the song to the section.
2. **Drums:**
   - a **backbeat verse kit**:
     - kick on 1, 2& and 3, with one extra per bar in a 4-bar order: none, 3a, 4a, then 1& + 3a;
     - a 4a pickup closing each 4-bar group in the pre-chorus;
     - snare on 2 and 4, sparse hats on 1e, 3e and 3a;
   - a **2-bar syncopated chorus kit**:
     - kick 1, 2a, 3, 4e (often 3e) │ 1, 3, 3e, 3&, with ghosts on 1a (and 2a);
     - snare on 2 and 4;
     - hats on every 16th except the "e" after the snare, open hats on the "&"s;
   - **3e/4e snare pickups** at phrase ends instead of tom fills;
   - a snare with a short tail in the verse and a longer one in the chorus;
   - a short 130 → 65 Hz kick.
3. **Bass, two riff styles that rest on beat 2:**
   - **verse:** a 2-bar riff on one chord: root, the fifth on 2&, the ♭7 below on 3 and above on
     4, the next root on 4a, and a chromatic approach from the leading tone on 4& of the second
     bar;
   - **chorus:** root on 1 and 1&, lower neighbour on 2&, root on 3, a 4&–4a pickup, a second bar
     that fills in, and a **chromatic walk-up** from VI into i;
   - the sound: F♯1–A♭2, octave-layered, dark (a low-pass around 400 Hz).
4. **Harmony:**
   - the **14-bar chorus** i i VII VII │ i i VII VII │ iv VI i i │ Vsus4 V;
   - a **verse vamp on the Dorian IV⁷** (major IV with ♭7, the voice adding ♯11);
   - a **pre-chorus stepping down by fifths over the IV pedal**, III/IV → VII/IV → IV → IV, twice;
   - substitutions it demonstrates: IV ↔ iv between sections, Vsus4 → V, and a 2-bar tag on i.
5. **Form blocks:**
   - a motif intro without drums;
   - a 4-bar drums-and-bass groove;
   - verse 8 + pre-chorus 8;
   - 14-bar choruses that run back to back;
   - an instrumental chorus with the voice out;
   - a 2-bar break with the bass out on V;
   - a tag on i;
   - a fade with the pads alone.
6. **Arrangement by addition:**
   - the verse is drums, bass and voice only;
   - pads and a 16th part enter in the pre-chorus;
   - the chorus adds the full pads, a mid-register 16th pulse on chord tones and the dense hats.

   on the 4th and 5th of the key, rising to the leading tone over V. Possibly also a low second

### The intro motif (bars 0–3)

The cleanest synth sample of the three records: the motif plays alone.

**Rhythm and contour:** a run of straight 16ths through bars 0 and 2 (16 notes a bar), a held
E♭5 in bars 1 and 3. The run climbs A♭4–E♭5 by step and restarts from A♭4, so each climb lands
on E♭5 and the cycle lands across the beat; the held E♭5 resolves it. Steps only, five notes.

**Tone** (solid; checked by rendering, clip 4b):

- **Three detuned saws.** On the held E♭5 the 1st harmonic splits into peaks across 630–641 Hz,
  the 2nd across 1259–1276 Hz and the 3rd across 1889–1911 Hz: the spread grows with the harmonic,
  so it's detuning, about 20–30 cents in all. The level beats at about 5 Hz. The peaks differ
  between left and right: a stereo ensemble or chorus.
- **A filter that closes on each note.** At the onset the harmonics sit near a plain saw's
  (h4 −4 dB, h8 −16, h12 −20). Within 0.3 s they settle to h4 −15, h6 −23, h8 −25, h12 −32: a
  saw through a 12 dB low-pass at about 3–3.5 kHz. Averaged over the motif, a saw through 6 kHz
  fits within 1.1 dB.
- **Attack:** from silence to within 9 dB of full in under 30 ms. The "soft attacks" of §7 are
  the lack of a click and the legato, not a slow rise.
- **Legato:** between 16ths the level never drops more than 3–4 dB. Each note runs into the next.
- **Tail:** the held E♭5 holds for 0.35 s (bar 1) or 1.2 s (bar 3), then falls about 15 dB a
  second, 24 dB in 1.6 s. That is slower than any release a 16th line would use: a long release
  plus a long reverb (RT60 about 3–4 s).
- **Echo:** none; the tail falls smoothly with no repeats.
- **Stereo:** L/R correlation 0.62–0.67, side 6–7 dB under mid: a little wider than the pad.

```ts
  // About 8 kHz at the onset, closing to 3.2 kHz within 0.3 s.
  filterEnvelope: {
    attack: 0.005,
    decay: 0.3,
    sustain: 0,
    release: 0.3,
    baseFrequency: 3200,
    octaves: 1.3,

}).chain(
  new Tone.Chorus({ frequency: 0.8, delayTime: 4, depth: 0.5, wet: 0.4 }).start(),

```

- Notes legato: each 16th lasts until the next starts.
- Reverb: a long send (the shared 3.2 s reverb at about 0.3).
- A fat oscillator starts its copies out of phase (faces.md §10.1). Here that only softens the
  first few ms, which suits "soft attacks".

### The chorus pulse (choruses 1–4 and the instrumental)

- **Register:** chord tones B♭3–F5. The strongest are B♭3, E♭4 and E♭5 on i, then F5: root and
  fifth in two octaves.
- **Rhythm:** **every 16th.** At 200–600 Hz a rise lands on 78–100% of each 16th, and the
  envelope peaks once per 16th, about 30 ms after it.
- **Envelope:** each hit falls 4–5 dB before the next: short decay over a sustained level, not
  a gated stab.
